What I care about

Who hears the alert.

At the Qualcomm Snapdragon Multiverse hackathon, Priyanka, Sharalee and I built Dhwani, which means "sound". It listens to machines the way a good technician does, through vibration, heat, sound and power, and catches faults days before a breakdown, all on the edge. We made the top 60 teams, then the final 6.

The part I care about most is who hears the alert: a technician on a loud factory floor who thinks in Kannada or Hindi, not English. So we used Sarvam to make Dhwani speak every alert in the operator's own language.

For most Indians, typing in English is a translation step. Voice removes it, but only if the software can hear how we really talk.

Mistakes I learned from

Where I got it wrong.

My parser was junk.

At Microsoft, my first parser for the procedure manual was a quick regex. It produced 84 rules, and 44 were junk: contents lines, duplicates and cross-references. I'd tried to shortcut the document instead of understanding it.

What I did nextI rebuilt it around the manual's chapter structure and got exactly 40 clean rules. Now I look at how the data is shaped before I touch a model.

My own test was rigged.

To test my Razorpay fraud model, I built a synthetic "digital twin" of a payments network. Then a plain tabular model, which can't see connections at all, caught about 94% of the fraud rings. That should have been impossible. My fake fraudsters were giving themselves away row by row: brand-new accounts with unusual amounts.

What I did nextI made each fraud account look normal on its own, matching ages and amounts to everyone else, so the only clue left was how they were connected. The tabular baseline dropped to an honest ~52%, and only the graph model still caught the rings. Now, when a number looks too good, I check whether my test is broken before I celebrate.
